Season CV is the résumé, playoffs included. For the regular season alone, the part most metrics and awards judge, rank by Regular season.

    Career CV is built on a player's five best seasons, with the playoffs weighing more than in a single season. Every other season adds a little, but only for what it gives above All-Star level.     What it measures

    Value in a real season. How much a player produced while he played, how much of the load he carried, whether his team won on the nights he played, and how well that team defended.

    Carrying a team counts. A player who shoulders a big share of the offense earns credit for that burden, even when his shooting is imperfect.

    What it doesn't claim

    • It isn't plus-minus or a causal impact estimate.
    • It isn't wins above replacement.
    • It doesn't rate talent, or who would have been best if healthy.
    • It doesn't settle career greatness.
    • Its defense is each team's real defense shared among its players, not a grade for each defender.

      How it works

      The draft. Each spin lands on a real team-season. Take one player who played at least half its games, and put him at his listed position or the one next to it (PG, SG, SF, PF, C). You get one skip a game. Each player can only be picked once, from any season.

      The rating. Your five play 34 minutes a night each and the bench is average. That's the same rule as the movie and Olympic teams in the Time Machine: each player's regular-season score (RS) from that season, measured against the average team of his own era. It reads as points per game better than an average team.

      The season. 82 games against real team-seasons from 1952 to today, half at home, using the Time Machine's game model (home court is worth about 4 points; a game's margin varies by about 12). Today's game gives everyone the same spins and the same schedule.

      The colors. Each pick's square shows his RS: 12+ all-time, 8–12 MVP range, 6–8 All-NBA, 4–6 All-Star, under 4.

      How it works · version 2.1.1

      How a season becomes a number

      Every step uses information recorded since 1952, and every season is measured against the other players in that same season. No era bonuses and no awards or reputation go into the score. ABA seasons carry one measured league-strength adjustment, estimated from players who moved between the leagues.

      1

      Count the production

      Points, assists and rebounds, the box-score numbers recorded every season since 1952. Efficient scoring counts for more than empty volume.

      2

      Weigh the load

      Players who carry more of their team's offense earn more of the credit for how the team did. Load measures responsibility, not skill.

      3

      Check the scoreboard

      How the team did in the games he actually played, and how the team did with him and without him, judged against what the players on the floor were expected to produce.

      4

      Share the defense

      Each team's real defense, rated the same way in every season since 1952, is shared among its players by their role on the team.

      5

      Then the playoffs

      Every playoff game is judged against the players who actually played it. Each postseason's team defense is measured on one common scale, each round counts once however long the series, short runs are read cautiously, and champions earn title credit by their share of the load.

      6

      One season score

      The regular season (RS) and the postseason (PO) combine into one CV. Missing the playoffs counts as league average for the playoff part, so a great season on a bad team still ranks.

      Two leagues, one scale

      The early ABA was a weaker league than the NBA, and it caught up. CV measures the gap from the players who moved between the leagues and adjusts each ABA season by its own year's gap. By 1975–76 the leagues were even.

      From seasons to careers

      Career CV is built on a player's five best seasons, with the playoffs counting more than they do in a single season. Every other season adds a little, but only for what it gives above All-Star level. A great prime decides it; long stretches of ordinary years don't. Decade CV and Franchise CV apply the same rule to a player's seasons inside one decade or with one franchise.

      Teams, Big 3s, decades and franchises

      From players to teams

      1

      Team rating

      Two views of each team are blended: its players' Court Value, weighted by minutes, and how the team actually did against its schedule. Results are measured against the spread of their own league, so a lopsided era gives no extra credit. The regular season counts 65% and the playoffs 35%, as in the season score.

      2

      Tested on the playoffs

      Every version was tested on all 4,819 playoff games and 933 series since 1952, each decade predicted from the others. Blending the roster with results picked series winners better than either alone, and its odds are well calibrated: 70–80% favorites won 77% of the time.

      3

      The Time Machine

      Series odds come from the two ratings, game by game, in a 2-2-1-1-1 format. Home court goes to the higher-rated team and is worth about four points, the edge measured in real playoff games. Single games carry the spread real playoff results show.

      4

      Big 3s

      A team's three best qualified seasons by Court Value, among players who spent at least 30% of that team's games there, added up. Regular season and playoffs count together.

      5

      All-Decade and All-Franchise teams

      Two guards, two forwards and a center per team, picked in order by Decade CV or Franchise CV. A player's position is the one he was listed at most in that decade or with that franchise.

      6

      Franchise histories

      Franchises keep their history through moves and renames, so the Lakers include Minneapolis and the Thunder include Seattle. The Nuggets, Pacers, Spurs and Nets keep their ABA years. A season counts for a franchise when the player was qualified and played at least 30% of its games.

      3

      Three is the magic number

      Three points equal one standard deviation within the season. A regular season of 9 sits about three standard deviations above that season's average player-game. In most years that's among the league's two or three best, whether the year is 1965 or 2015.

      Checking the work

      How CV lines up

      CV never looks at awards or at other metrics, so both are fair checks. They judge only the regular season, so the fair comparison is CV's regular-season score (RS). RS matches the All-NBA teams better than any modern metric, and agrees with the metrics about as closely as the voters do. Season CV adds the playoffs, which none of them judge, so it agrees a little less.

      Known limits

      • Box scores before 1973–74 have no steals, blocks or turnovers, so CV uses none in any era.
      • Defense is each team's real defense shared among players by role; it is not a grade for each defender.
      • Standing within a season doesn't prove equal strength across eras or leagues.
      • Some early playoff box scores (NBA before 1965, ABA before 1972) are missing assists; those games are estimated and marked "Partial playoff box".
      • Players who almost never missed a game give the with-and-without comparison little to work with.
      • A player traded mid-season carries his whole-season score to each team in Big 3s, decades and franchise lists.
      • Records and titles start in 1951–52, so earlier titles (the Minneapolis Lakers' 1949 and 1950) are not counted. A few teams with gaps in their game records show no win–loss line.
